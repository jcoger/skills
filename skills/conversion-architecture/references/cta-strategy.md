# CTA Strategy

What the page asks, how often, in what words, and what happens after the click.

---

## One primary action

Published research is consistent: pages with a single CTA convert around 13.5% on average versus roughly 10.5% for pages with five or more. The mechanism is decision cost: every additional distinct ask makes the visitor choose between offers instead of choosing the offer.

Rules:
- **One primary action per page**, defined before any design: trial, demo, purchase, signup, call. This is the page's conversion; everything serves it.
- **Repeat, do not compete.** The same primary CTA appears at rhythm (hero, after major proof beats, close). Repetition of one ask is discipline; variety of asks is paralysis.
- **At most one alternate, one rung lower.** A lower-commitment same-direction option ("View demo" beside "Start free trial", "See the migration guide" beside "Start migration"). The alternate catches visitors not ready for the primary, without changing direction.
- **Navigation is not exempt.** On paid-traffic landers, strip or minimize nav; every header link is a competing CTA. (Organic pages keep nav; see the site architecture skill's canonical rules.)

## The commitment ladder

Match the ask to the audience temperature:

| Temperature | Appropriate primary ask |
|---|---|
| Cold | Low commitment: read more, get the guide, take the quiz, see how it works |
| Warm | Medium: start free trial, see a demo, try the tool |
| Hot | High: buy, book, start migration, talk to sales today |

Asking cold traffic to "Book a call" skips rungs and converts almost nobody; asking hot traffic to "Learn more" wastes intent it already has.

## Label copy

- **Say the value or the outcome, not the mechanism.** "Start resolving tickets today" beats "Submit". A widely cited test: changing "Submit" to "Send Message" lifted conversions about 20%; the principle is that labels naming the action's meaning outperform generic verbs.
- **First person often wins:** "Start my trial" / "Get my plan" tests well in many contexts; keep voice consistent across the page.
- **Set expectations in or beside the button:** "Free 14-day trial. No credit card." The microcopy near the CTA is the highest-leverage copy on the page.
- **Never label dishonestly.** "Get instant access" that leads to a sales-call form is a trust burn that poisons the brand beyond this page.

## Placement rhythm

- First viewport: always.
- After each major proof beat: the moments of peak belief are the moments to ask.
- The close: full-width, restating the offer and risk reversal, not just a button on a colored band.
- Long pages: a CTA roughly every 2-3 viewports; sticky CTAs on mobile earn their place on hot-traffic pages.

## Forms

- **Intent beats field count.** The old rule (every field above three kills conversion) belongs to the low-intent lead-gen era. High-intent demand-gen pages run long forms successfully; well-qualified visitors will complete 8 fields when the value is clear. The real rule: ask only what sales or the product genuinely needs at this step, and let intent set the budget.
- Label fields honestly, mark optional ones, never ask twice.
- Multi-step beats one wall for longer forms: easy fields first, commitment builds.
- State what happens after submission, next to the button ("We reply within one business day").

## Post-click honesty

The CTA is a promise. The click must land where the label said: "Start free trial" goes to signup, not a contact form; "View pricing" shows numbers, not a quote request. Every broken promise at this layer costs more than the page's persuasion earned.
