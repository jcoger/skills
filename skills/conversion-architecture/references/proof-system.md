# Proof System

What counts as proof, where it sits, and how much a page needs. Social proof is the strongest conversion lever in published research; most pages still use the weakest forms of it.

---

## The proof hierarchy (strongest first)

1. **Specific-outcome testimonial with name, role, company.** "Fin resolves 65% of our support volume" beats any adjective. The number plus the name is the unit of proof.
2. **Segment-matched case study.** The reader's industry, size, and problem. A mismatched case study (enterprise logo for an SMB visitor) proves less than a smaller matched one.
3. **Aggregate outcome data.** "Customers cut response time 40% on average." Strong when honest about the sample.
4. **Scale numbers.** "25,000+ brands", "4M sites". Proves adoption, not outcomes; good early-trust signal, weak close.
5. **Expert or known-voice endorsement.** Borrowed authority; strongest with cold traffic that does not know you.
6. **Logo band.** Fast recognition trust. Table stakes for B2B; proves nothing specific.
7. **Badges, ratings, certifications.** (G2, SOC 2, star ratings.) Peripheral trust; useful near forms and money asks.

Anonymous praise ("Amazing tool!" - happy customer) sits below all of these. It counts as zero and can count negative; visitors read it as fabricated.

## Placement rules

- **One-viewport rule:** proof lives within one viewport of the claim it supports. Claim here, evidence two scrolls away = claim discounted.
- **Match proof to claim:** a speed claim gets a speed number; a support-quality claim gets a support quote. Generic praise next to a specific claim proves the wrong thing.
- **First-viewport trust signal:** every page carries one fast trust marker before the fold (logo band, scale number, rating).
- **Proof at the money:** the beat where you ask (pricing table, form, close) always has proof adjacent. Anxiety peaks at the ask.
- **Persona-matched filtering:** on persona and solutions pages, filter logos and quotes to that segment. Proof from "their world" multiplies.

## Density by price and risk tier

| Tier | Example | Minimum proof load |
|---|---|---|
| Low ($0-50, self-serve) | Consumer app, cheap tool | Rating + a few real quotes + scale number |
| Mid ($50-500/mo, team buy) | Team SaaS | Logo band + 3 specific-outcome testimonials + 1 matched case study + badges at the form |
| High ($500-5k/mo, manager approval) | Department platform | All of the above + case studies per key segment + security/compliance answers + named references on request |
| Enterprise ($50k+, committee) | Platform deal | Proof becomes its own cluster: customers hub, analyst mentions, security center, reference program. The page links into it |

Underproofing an expensive thing kills it quietly (visitors leave without telling you why). Overproofing a cheap thing creates suspicion (why does a $9 tool need this much defense?).

## Specificity rules

- Numbers beat adjectives. "Cut onboarding from 6 weeks to 9 days" beats "dramatically faster onboarding".
- Odd, precise numbers read truer than round ones (67%, not 70%; published research on this is consistent).
- Name, role, company, photo where possible. Each removed element costs believability.
- Friction-honest quotes outperform pure praise: "setup took a weekend, then it just worked" reads human; perfection reads scripted.
- Date proof when recency matters; a 2019 quote about an AI feature is anti-proof.

## Failure modes

- **The press-release quote.** "We are thrilled to partner with..." proves a partnership exists, not that anything works.
- **The wall of logos with no story.** Twenty logos and zero quotes reads as a sales deck, not evidence.
- **Self-proof.** "Rated #1 by our own report." Worse than nothing.
- **Fabricated specificity.** Never invent numbers, names, or reviews; beyond ethics, fake ratings markup risks platform and legal action. If real proof does not exist yet, use honest scale or founder-direct framing ("we are early; here is exactly what we will do for you") instead.
