# Offer Framing

The offer is not the product; it is the deal: what they get, what it costs them in money, time, and effort, what protects them if it goes wrong, and why acting now is rational. Pages without a framed offer ask visitors to want the product in the abstract.

---

## The four-part offer

Every conversion spec states all four:

1. **Get:** the outcome in the visitor's terms, concrete enough to picture. "Your support inbox at zero by Friday" not "streamlined workflows".
2. **Cost, fully:** money AND time AND effort. Hiding the time/effort cost converts a click and churns a customer. Effort honesty ("setup takes about an hour") paradoxically lifts conversion with serious buyers because it reads true.
3. **Risk reversal:** what protects them. Free trial, money-back window, cancel anytime, free migration, keep-your-data export. Pick the strongest one you can actually honor and say it at the close.
4. **Why now:** a real reason or nothing. See honest urgency below.

## Value framing moves

- **Anchor against the real alternative:** the incumbent's price, the cost of the problem (hours lost, deals missed), or the done-for-you alternative. "$99/mo" floats; "$99/mo replaces the $2k/mo agency retainer" lands.
- **Denominate in their unit:** per seat, per resolved ticket, per project. The unit should make the value obvious, not obscure it.
- **Name the plan for the buyer, not the tier:** "For growing teams" beats "Pro". One plan visibly highlighted as the default choice.
- **Concrete beats complete:** three sharply described things beat ten vague ones. The feature list is for the comparison table, not the offer.

## Risk reversal menu (strongest you can honor)

| Reversal | When it fits |
|---|---|
| Free trial, no card | Self-serve, fast time-to-value |
| Money-back window | Paid-upfront products confident in week-one value |
| Cancel anytime, monthly | Subscription default; say it explicitly |
| Free or assisted migration | Switcher audiences; kills the effort objection too |
| Pilot / proof-of-concept | Enterprise; structured de-risking |
| Keep your data / easy export | Counters lock-in fear; rare and powerful because honest |

Restate the chosen reversal at the close, beside the final CTA, where anxiety peaks.

## Honest urgency (the only menu)

Urgency tied to real events lifts conversion substantially (studies report up to 40% on authentic deadline offers). Fake urgency does the opposite once detected, and detection is the norm now; evergreen countdowns and false scarcity are documented dark patterns drawing regulatory action in multiple markets.

Real urgency sources:
- **Real deadlines:** price change on a date, plan grandfathering, enrollment windows, event dates.
- **The visitor's own deadline:** their renewal date, their season, their launch. (The strongest urgency is theirs, not yours.)
- **Real capacity:** genuinely limited seats, cohorts, inventory, onboarding slots, stated plainly.
- **Real calendar relevance:** seasonal offers with actual ends.
- **Cost of waiting:** honest arithmetic of what the problem costs per month unsolved.

Never: evergreen countdown timers that reset, "only 2 left" that is always 2, fake "sale ends tonight" that repeats nightly. If there is no real reason to act now, the close runs on value and risk reversal alone, and that is fine.

## Next-step transparency

The offer includes what happens after the click: trial length and what is in it, who contacts them and when, how long setup takes, when they see first value. Buyers at the decision point are buying the next 14 days, not the feature list. Spell them out: "Start your trial -> import your data in 10 minutes -> your first report tomorrow morning."

## Failure modes

- **The unnamed offer:** a page that describes a product and ends with "Get started" has framed no deal at all.
- **Hidden costs surfacing later:** setup fees, annual-only billing, per-seat surprises. Every hidden cost is a churn seed and a review risk.
- **Urgency theater:** see above; the conversion it gains is borrowed from the brand at punitive interest.
- **Risk reversal you cannot honor:** a guarantee with fine-print escape hatches reads as a trap and converts like one.
